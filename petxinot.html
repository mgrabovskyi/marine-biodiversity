The Iberian Petxinot

An endangered species we talked about with the oceanographer.

The Iberian “Petxinot” is a freshwater mussel native to eastern Spain. Here is what we learned about how it lives, why it is endangered, and how it is being protected.

Three Endangered Iberian Freshwater Mussels

At the Oceanogràfic, we saw three species of freshwater mussels that are all listed as “in danger of extinction” in the Valencian catalog of threatened fauna. They are Unio mancus (about 8 cm), Anodonta anatina (about 15 cm), and Potomida littoralis (about 6 cm). All three are part of the recovery plan led by the Generalitat Valenciana, with support from the European Maritime and Fisheries Fund.

Why Are They Called Náyades?

Even though we usually call these animals “petxinots,” this group of species is also called náyades (naiads) — the same name given to the nymphs that protected the rivers in Greek mythology. Curiously, these bivalves filter and purify the water, so their presence is an indicator of water quality and the balance of the ecosystem. Coincidence, or poetry?

A Unique Life Cycle

Petxinots have a very unusual life cycle. During part of their lives they are actually parasites of fish. Their larval stage is called a glochid (or gloquidio in Spanish). The tiny glochids attach themselves to the gills and fins of a host fish — often a barbel — and feed there while they grow. After some time, they detach, fall to the bottom of the river or lagoon, and spend the rest of their lives there as filter feeders.

Our Questions for the Oceanographer

1. How does it reproduce?

The Iberian petxinot reproduces in a very unusual way. The female releases tiny larvae into the water, and these larvae must attach themselves to the gills of a fish, usually a barbel. While attached, they receive nutrients and protection as they grow. After some time, they detach from the fish and continue living independently on the bottom of rivers or lagoons.

2. At what age are they reintroduced into their habitat?

Petxinots can be reintroduced into their natural habitat at different stages of life, from young juveniles to older adults. However, scientists have found that older individuals survive more easily because they are larger, stronger, and more resistant to predators and environmental changes.

3. How many specimens are there now?

Today, there are approximately 13,000 Iberian petxinots remaining. Although this number is higher than in previous years thanks to conservation programs, the species is still considered vulnerable and needs continued protection to avoid another population decline.

4. What do they eat?

The Iberian petxinot feeds mainly on plankton and tiny organic particles found in the water. It is a filter feeder, meaning it filters water through its body and removes microscopic food particles. By doing this, it also helps keep the water cleaner and healthier for other species.

5. At what population level was it considered endangered?

The species was considered endangered when its population dropped to around 5,000 individuals. At that time, habitat destruction, pollution, and invasive species had greatly reduced its numbers, putting the petxinot at serious risk of extinction.

6. What threats does it face in the wild?

The Iberian petxinot faces several threats in the wild. The most important are invasive species such as the zebra mussel and the Atlantic oyster, which compete for food and space. Pollution, habitat loss, changes in water quality, and climate change also threaten its survival by damaging the ecosystems where it lives.

7. What can we do to help it?

People can help the petxinot by protecting wetlands and rivers, reducing pollution, and supporting conservation projects. Raising awareness is also very important because many people do not know this species exists or understand its ecological importance. Education can encourage communities to protect local biodiversity.

8. How old are they when they reach adulthood?

The Iberian petxinot grows very slowly. It usually reaches adulthood after 20 to 30 years. This slow growth means that populations recover very slowly if many individuals are lost.

9. At what age do they reach their full potential?

They reach their full size and reproductive potential when they are older adults. Older individuals are especially important because they are often stronger, produce more offspring, and contribute more to the stability of the population.

10. What other animals in Albufera are researchers helping?

Apart from the petxinot, scientists and conservationists are also working to protect other endangered native species such as the fartet and the samaruc, two small freshwater fish that are unique to eastern Spain. These species are threatened by pollution, habitat destruction, invasive species, and changes in water quality.
