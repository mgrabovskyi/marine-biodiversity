Our First Line of Inquiry

The diversity of marine species and their characteristics — a deep dive below.

Marine biodiversity is the enormous variety of life that exists in the oceans, seas, and coastal ecosystems of our planet. It includes every form of marine life, from microscopic phytoplankton and algae to giant animals such as the Blue Whale, the largest animal ever known to exist. Marine biodiversity also includes fish, corals, crustaceans, mollusks, marine mammals, sea turtles, sharks, seabirds, and countless other organisms that interact in complex ecosystems. These ecosystems are essential for life on Earth because they regulate the climate, produce oxygen, absorb carbon dioxide, and provide food and resources for billions of people.

The ocean is the largest habitat on Earth, covering more than 70% of the planet's surface and containing approximately 80% of all known biodiversity. Scientists have officially identified around 248,900 marine species, but many researchers believe that the real number may be close to 2 million species or even higher. This means that humans may have discovered only between 10% and 25% of all marine life. Large parts of the deep ocean remain unexplored, and new species are still being discovered every year in underwater caves, hydrothermal vents, and deep-sea trenches.

Fun fact: Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but support around 25% of all marine species. That is why they are often called the “rainforests of the sea.”

Marine biodiversity is not distributed evenly across the oceans. Certain areas, known as biodiversity “hotspots,” contain exceptionally high numbers of species. One of the most important examples is coral reefs, often called the “rainforests of the sea.” Although coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor, they support around 25% of all marine species. Reefs provide shelter, food, and breeding grounds for fish, crustaceans, and countless other organisms. Other critical ecosystems include mangrove forests, seagrass meadows, estuaries, salt marshes, and kelp forests. Mangroves protect coastlines from storms and erosion, while seagrasses and kelp forests store huge amounts of carbon and provide nursery habitats for young fish and shellfish.

Marine biodiversity is also directly connected to human survival and economic stability. More than 3 billion people depend on seafood as a major source of protein, and millions of jobs rely on fishing, tourism, shipping, and marine industries. Healthy oceans also play a major role in regulating the Earth's climate by absorbing about 30% of the carbon dioxide produced by human activities and capturing more than 90% of the excess heat caused by global warming. Without the ocean acting as a climate regulator, climate change on land would be even more severe.

Despite its importance, marine biodiversity is declining at an alarming rate. Over the past century, human activities have caused rapid and unprecedented damage to marine ecosystems. Scientists warn that Earth may now be entering a “sixth mass extinction,” with extinction rates estimated to be between 100 and 1,000 times higher than natural background levels before large-scale human impact.

Overfishing

One of the greatest threats to marine biodiversity is overfishing. According to recent estimates, about 37.7% of global fish stocks are overfished, meaning fish are being removed from the ocean faster than populations can recover naturally. Industrial fishing methods such as bottom trawling destroy entire seafloor habitats by dragging heavy nets across the ocean floor, damaging coral reefs, sponges, and breeding grounds. Since 1970, shark and ray populations have declined by around 71%, largely because of overfishing and accidental capture. Many top predators are disappearing, which disrupts the balance of marine food chains and weakens ecosystem stability.

Climate Change

Climate change is another major driver of biodiversity loss in the oceans. Global sea temperatures continue to rise, and in 2024 average sea surface temperatures reached a record 21°C. Warmer waters place enormous stress on marine organisms, especially corals. Coral bleaching occurs when corals expel the algae living inside them due to heat stress, often leading to mass coral death if temperatures remain high for too long. Rising temperatures also reduce oxygen levels in seawater, creating conditions where many marine species struggle to survive.

Ocean Acidification

At the same time, the ocean is becoming more acidic because it absorbs increasing amounts of atmospheric carbon dioxide. Over the last century, ocean acidity has increased by approximately 30%. Acidification weakens the ability of corals, oysters, mussels, and other shell-forming organisms to build and maintain their skeletons and shells. Since these species form the basis of many marine food webs, their decline can affect entire ecosystems.

Pollution

Pollution is another serious threat to marine biodiversity. Every year, around 8 million tons of plastic waste enter the oceans. Plastic pollution affects more than 2,000 marine species through ingestion, entanglement, or habitat contamination. Sea turtles often mistake plastic bags for jellyfish, seabirds feed plastic fragments to their chicks, and whales have been found with large quantities of plastic in their stomachs. In addition to plastics, chemical pollution, oil spills, and untreated sewage damage marine ecosystems and poison wildlife.

Agricultural runoff also contributes to ocean degradation. Fertilizers rich in nitrogen and phosphorus wash into rivers and eventually reach the sea, where they trigger excessive algae growth. When the algae die and decompose, oxygen levels in the water drop dramatically, creating “dead zones” where very few organisms can survive. Some dead zones have become so large that they cover thousands of square kilometers.

Habitat Destruction

Habitat destruction has accelerated biodiversity loss even further. Since 1870, humans have destroyed around 50% of the world's coral reefs and approximately 40% of mangrove forests. Coastal development, tourism infrastructure, dredging, and pollution continue to damage fragile ecosystems that many species rely on for breeding and shelter.

Invasive Species

Invasive species are also becoming more common due to global trade, shipping, and warming seas. Organisms transported in ballast water or attached to ships can spread into new environments where they outcompete native species. One example is the Atlantic blue crab, which has caused severe declines in local shellfish populations in some regions. Invasive species often disrupt food webs and reduce native biodiversity.

A Triple Planetary Crisis — But Still Hope

The United Nations describes the current situation as a “triple planetary crisis” involving climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution. Together, these problems place enormous pressure on marine ecosystems and threaten the services the ocean provides to humanity.

However, there is still hope. Marine ecosystems can recover surprisingly quickly when given proper protection. Marine protected areas, sustainable fishing practices, habitat restoration, pollution reduction, and climate action have already shown positive results in many parts of the world. Some whale populations, for example, have partially recovered after international hunting bans, and certain coral reefs have shown resilience when local pollution and overfishing are reduced.

Protecting marine biodiversity is not only about saving individual species; it is about preserving the balance of life on Earth. Healthy oceans regulate climate, support food systems, protect coastlines, and sustain millions of livelihoods. The future of humanity is deeply connected to the health of the ocean, making marine conservation one of the most important global challenges of the 21st century.
