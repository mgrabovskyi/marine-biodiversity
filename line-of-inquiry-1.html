Our First Line of Inquiry

The diversity of marine species and their characteristics.

The ocean is the largest habitat on Earth — and we have only discovered a small fraction of the life inside it.

What Lives in the Sea?

Marine biodiversity is the enormous variety of life that exists in the oceans, seas, and coastal ecosystems of our planet. It includes every form of marine life, from microscopic phytoplankton and algae to giant animals such as the Blue Whale, the largest animal ever known to exist. It also includes fish, corals, crustaceans, mollusks, marine mammals, sea turtles, sharks, seabirds, and countless other organisms that interact in complex ecosystems.

80% of known biodiversity is in the ocean
248,900 marine species officially identified
~2M species scientists think exist in total
10–25% of marine life has been discovered

The ocean covers more than 70% of the planet's surface. New species are still being discovered every year in underwater caves, hydrothermal vents, and deep-sea trenches.

Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but support around 25% of all marine species. That is why they are often called the “rainforests of the sea.”

Biodiversity Hotspots

Marine biodiversity is not distributed evenly across the oceans. Certain areas, known as biodiversity hotspots, contain exceptionally high numbers of species.

Coral Reefs

Shelter, food, and breeding grounds for fish, crustaceans, and countless other organisms.

Mangrove Forests

Protect coastlines from storms and erosion. Nursery habitats for young fish and shrimp.

Seagrass Meadows

Store huge amounts of carbon and provide nursery habitats for many species.

Kelp Forests

Cold-water giants that house fish, crabs, sea otters, and lots of small invertebrates.

Why Humans Depend on It

Marine biodiversity is directly connected to human survival and economic stability. More than 3 billion people depend on seafood as a major source of protein, and millions of jobs rely on fishing, tourism, shipping, and marine industries.

Healthy oceans also play a major role in regulating Earth's climate by absorbing about 30% of the carbon dioxide produced by human activities and capturing more than 90% of the excess heat caused by global warming. Without the ocean acting as a climate regulator, climate change on land would be even more severe.

A Sixth Mass Extinction?

Despite its importance, marine biodiversity is declining at an alarming rate. Scientists warn that Earth may now be entering a sixth mass extinction, with extinction rates estimated to be 100 to 1,000 times higher than natural background levels before large-scale human impact.

8M tons of plastic enter the ocean each year

Overfishing

One of the greatest threats to marine biodiversity is overfishing. About 37.7% of global fish stocks are overfished — meaning fish are being removed from the ocean faster than populations can recover naturally. Industrial methods such as bottom trawling destroy entire seafloor habitats by dragging heavy nets across the ocean floor, damaging coral reefs, sponges, and breeding grounds.

Since 1970, shark and ray populations have declined by around 71%, largely because of overfishing and accidental capture. When top predators disappear, the balance of marine food chains breaks down and ecosystem stability weakens.

Climate Change

Climate change is another major driver of biodiversity loss in the oceans. Global sea temperatures continue to rise — in 2024, average sea surface temperatures reached a record 21°C. Warmer waters place enormous stress on marine organisms, especially corals.

Coral bleaching occurs when corals expel the algae living inside them due to heat stress, often leading to mass coral death if temperatures remain high for too long. Rising temperatures also reduce oxygen levels in seawater.

Ocean Acidification

The ocean is becoming more acidic because it absorbs increasing amounts of atmospheric carbon dioxide. Over the last century, ocean acidity has increased by approximately 30%. Acidification weakens the ability of corals, oysters, mussels, and other shell-forming organisms to build and maintain their skeletons and shells.

Pollution

Every year, around 8 million tons of plastic waste enter the oceans. Plastic pollution affects more than 2,000 marine species through ingestion, entanglement, or habitat contamination.

Sea turtles often mistake plastic bags for jellyfish, seabirds feed plastic fragments to their chicks, and whales have been found with large quantities of plastic in their stomachs.

Agricultural runoff also contributes to ocean degradation. Fertilizers rich in nitrogen and phosphorus wash into rivers and reach the sea, triggering excessive algae growth. When the algae die and decompose, oxygen levels in the water drop dramatically, creating dead zones where very few organisms can survive.

Habitat Destruction & Invasive Species

Since 1870, humans have destroyed around 50% of the world's coral reefs and approximately 40% of mangrove forests. Coastal development, tourism infrastructure, dredging, and pollution continue to damage fragile ecosystems.

Invasive species are also becoming more common due to global trade, shipping, and warming seas. Organisms transported in ballast water or attached to ships spread into new environments where they outcompete native species. One example is the Atlantic blue crab, which has caused severe declines in local shellfish populations.

There Is Still Hope

The United Nations describes the current situation as a triple planetary crisis: climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution. But marine ecosystems can recover surprisingly quickly when given proper protection. Marine protected areas, sustainable fishing, habitat restoration, pollution reduction, and climate action have already shown positive results.

Some whale populations have partially recovered after international hunting bans. Certain coral reefs have shown resilience when local pollution and overfishing are reduced.

Key Takeaway

Protecting marine biodiversity is about preserving the balance of life on Earth. Healthy oceans regulate climate, support food systems, protect coastlines, and sustain millions of livelihoods.
